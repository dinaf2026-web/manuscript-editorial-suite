# Immersion Checklist — Line Craft Reference

The bar is the project's `craft_standard` (a technique target, never borrowed
sentences) at the book's target `audience` and `register`. This file is the deep
rubric the audit runs against — genre-general, applies to any narrative prose.

---

## The Four Systems (every scene must carry all four)

**1. Character continuity.** People arrive carrying their history; relationships
have changed since the last scene. Grief evolves; alliances shift; trust grows or
frays book to book. *Leak:* characters reset to a neutral state each scene, as if
meeting for the first time.

**2. Layered dialogue.** Subtext is always present; characters rarely say exactly
what they mean. A parent withholds fear to stay strong; a rival respects competence
without admitting it; cruelty covers envy. *Leak:* on-the-nose lines where everyone
states their feelings and intentions plainly.

**3. Environmental presence.** The setting is alive and pressing on the scene —
weather, sound, texture, smell, history — filtered through the POV character's
mood, not catalogued. *Leak:* the anywhere-room; a setting that could be deleted
with no loss.

**4. Emotional thread.** A personal stake runs *under* every action beat. *Leak:*
plot mechanics with the feeling announced afterward, or not at all. Master test:
*What is the POV character feeling underneath the action?* If unanswerable, the
scene isn't immersive yet.

## Line-level tells to hunt

### Information dialogue (the #1 draft-level failure)
Characters telling each other things they both already know, for the reader's
benefit.
- *Tell:* "As you know, your father died in the crash when you were eight." → both
  characters know this; it's exposition wearing a dialogue costume.
- *Fix direction:* delete the known facts; let the scene reveal them through
  behavior, withholding, or a detail only one character reacts to. Convert
  exposition into character.

### Furniture-inventory description
Setting listed like an estate-agent walkthrough instead of seen through a person.
- *Tell:* three sentences naming every object in the room in order.
- *Fix direction:* choose the one or two details the POV character's eye and mood
  would actually catch right now; let the rest stay implied. Description should do
  emotional work.

### Talking heads
Dialogue floating with no bodies in space.
- *Tell:* 5+ consecutive exchanges with no movement, gesture, or physical beat.
- *Fix direction:* ground the speakers — what their hands do, where they look, the
  pause before the hard line. Bodies carry subtext words can't.

### Missing micro-reactions  (the single biggest unlock)
A movement, thought, or physical reaction every 2–3 dialogue lines.
- *Tell:* long stretches of pure speech; the reader loses the room.
- *Fix direction:* add the small beat — a tilt of the head, a held breath, a jaw
  tightening — that shows reaction and keeps the scene physical.

### Filter words (POV distancers)
"saw / felt / noticed / realized / heard / watched / seemed" that put a pane of
glass between the reader and the POV character.
- *Tell:* "She saw the door swing open." / "She felt afraid."
- *Fix direction:* drop the filter and render the thing directly — "The door swung
  open." / let the fear show in body and thought. Reserve filters for when the act
  of perceiving is itself the point.

### Emotion stated, not threaded
The feeling labeled after the beat instead of lived inside it.
- *Tell:* an action, then "She felt a wave of fear."
- *Fix direction:* move the stake *into* the action — the fear shapes what she does
  in the beat, so it never needs naming.

### Off-register drift
- *Tells:* prose that slips below or above the target reading level — over-
  explaining a joke or a feeling, low subtext, tidy resolutions where tension
  belongs, or sentence music that talks down to (or over the head of) the intended
  reader.
- *Fix direction:* trust the target reader to infer; raise the subtext; let
  discomfort sit. Match the profile's `register` and `audience`.

### Tell-don't-trust
Spelling out what the reader could deduce from what's already on the page.
- *Fix direction:* cut the explanation; the inference is the reader's pleasure.

## Recurring anchors to deploy (the 5th upgrade)
Recurring locations, habits, objects, and dynamics that make a world feel
lived-in. Reaching for an established anchor is often the cheapest way to lift a
flat scene. (If the book has a bible or canon ledger, its recurring anchors are
listed there.)

## The Five Upgrades (every draft pass)
1. **Kill information dialogue** → transform it to reveal character.
2. **Add micro-reactions** → the single biggest immersion unlock.
3. **Build character friction** → mild disagreement, subtle irritation; no
   convenient cooperation.
4. **Thread emotion through action** → the stake becomes personal *inside* the
   beat, not after.
5. **Use recurring anchors** → locations, habits, dynamics the world owns.

## Draft-level failure patterns (the cautionary baseline — keep them out)
Information dialogue · furniture-inventory setting · off-register tone · convenient
cooperation between characters · talking heads · feelings narrated instead of
dramatized.
