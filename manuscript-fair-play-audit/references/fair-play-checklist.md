# Fair-Play Checklist — Mystery Construction

The fair-play tradition (Knox, Van Dine) holds that the reader must have a real
chance to solve the mystery: clues shown, no withheld facts, no supernatural
shortcuts, no solution from coincidence. Adapt the *spirit*, not the dated letter,
for contemporary fiction. The rule that governs above all: **no coincidence solves
the plot, ever.**

## Core principles (adapted)

1. **The culprit and the key clues appear early.** The reader meets the solution's
   pieces well before the reveal — even if their meaning is hidden.
2. **No withheld information.** The detective cannot solve it on facts the reader
   never saw. Whatever the sleuth observes or deduces must be *on the page* when
   made, not produced retroactively at the reveal.
3. **No coincidence as solution.** Luck may *cause trouble* (a missed train, a
   storm) but must never *solve* the case. Resolution comes from a character's
   choice or deduction. This is the hardest rule — enforce it.
4. **No out-of-character action for plot convenience.** If the case only works
   because someone behaves against established character, the construction is
   broken.
5. **Red herrings must be fair and resolved.** A misleading clue is fine if an
   honest reader could be misled and the herring is explained by the end.
6. **The solution is surprising AND inevitable.** Surprise without inevitability
   feels like a cheat; inevitability without surprise feels flat. Both, or revise.

## Clue-type taxonomy (label clues in the map)

- **Physical** — an object, mark, or trace (a birthmark, a logbook, a fiber).
- **Testimonial** — something a character says (or pointedly doesn't).
- **Behavioral** — a tell or action (a nervous habit, an animal's reaction).
- **Sensory** — what a character sees, hears, or smells that others miss.
- **Absence** — the dog that didn't bark: something that *should* be present isn't.
- **Sequencing** — timeline / alibi logic.

A healthy case mixes types and distributes them across chapters, not clustered at
the end.

## Plant-and-payoff discipline

- Every reveal needs an earlier **plant** the reader could have caught.
- Every prominent **plant** should pay off — orphaned plants read as loose ends
  (cut them or pay them off).
- **Series-level plants** (a long arc across books) may plant now and pay off books
  later — that's intended patience, not an orphan. Distinguish local from series
  plants (the profile / canon ledger records the long arc, if any).
- The best plants do double duty: meaningless locally, meaningful in hindsight.

## Detective-method discipline

- If the profile defines *how* the sleuth reasons (a specific method, a partnership
  division of labor, a signature approach), the solution must be reached *that*
  way. A sleuth who suddenly reasons out of character to reach the answer is a
  fairness break, not a clever twist.
- A clue source that is not a solver (an informant, an animal, a database) must
  hand the detective something to *interpret* — it must never conveniently produce
  the finished answer.

## Register cautions

- **Cozy / lighter register:** peril is episodic; the puzzle, not gore, carries
  tension; violence is typically off-page. Keep stakes real but proportioned.
- **Thriller / darker register:** pace and threat run higher, but fairness still
  binds — a twist that cheats the reader is a defect at any intensity.

## Common construction failures to flag

- Reveal-only facts (information that appears for the first time at the solution).
- "Detective just knows" — intuition standing in for a shown clue.
- Coincidental discovery doing the work a deduction should.
- A herring introduced and never cleared.
- Villain confession that explains clues the reader was never given.
- A midpoint that doesn't recontextualize earlier clues.
